# The Cosmic Speed Limit

*Why nothing beats light, and the real ways physics bends around it*

N365 Sciences · Mr. Pierre Feynman Curie · November 12, 2026 · v3 · CHURCH (Nov 13, 2026)

**The short version.** Light speed (299,792,458 meters per second, about 186,000 miles per second) isn't really a rule about light. It's the top speed of cause and effect, built into how space and time fit together. Light reaches it only because light has no mass. Nothing with mass can reach it, and no message can beat it. But there are honest ways to shorten a trip, and some wild-but-legal loopholes on paper.

## 1. Why there's a limit at all

**Reason one: the energy bill.** Push on something and it speeds up. At everyday speeds, doubling the push roughly doubles the result. Near light speed that stops working: more and more of your energy goes into making the object harder to push, not faster. The bill goes up without end as you near light speed. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

**Example.** Take 1 kilogram (about a bag of sugar). Getting it to 90% of light speed takes about 120 quadrillion joules (1.2 × 10^17), roughly 1.7 hours of the whole world's primary energy use (2023). To reach 99.9% you'd need about 16 times that, and at 100% the bill is infinite. Newton's old formula says you could just keep going, and that's where it fails.

**Reason two: cause and effect.** Draw space across and time up the page. Light coming out of 'you, now' makes a cone. Everything inside the top cone is your future, and everything inside the bottom cone is your past. A faster-than-light message would land in the 'elsewhere' zone. For some moving observers, that message arrives before it was sent, so you could answer a question before it was asked. The limit keeps the order of events the same for everyone. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

## 2. The time trick (real, measured, and the best way we have)

You can't beat light, but near light speed your own clock slows down compared with people at home. The ship never breaks the speed limit. The crew just experiences less time on the way. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

**Measured, not just theory.** In 2010 NIST compared two aluminum-ion atomic clocks. A clock whose ion was jiggled at a few meters per second (car-like speeds) ticked slightly slower than the still one, exactly as relativity predicts (Chou et al., Science, Sept. 24, 2010).

**Example.** Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun, is 4.24 light-years away (Gaia DR3). At 99.9% of light speed, Earth waits about 4.24 years, but the crew ages only about 0.19 years, roughly 10 weeks. Stretch that to a star 100 light-years out and the crew ages about 4.5 years while Earth waits just over 100. The catch is getting there: Chart 2 shows the energy, and nobody can build that engine today.

## 3. Relativity in your pocket: GPS

You use relativity every time your phone finds you on a map. GPS works by timing radio signals from satellites about 20,200 km (12,550 miles) up. Your phone measures how long each signal took to arrive, and since the signal travels at light speed, the time tells it the distance. Get the timing a little wrong and you get the distance wrong. Up there, two effects from this piece change how fast the satellite clocks tick. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

**Speed slows the clock.** The satellites move at about 3.9 km per second, so the time trick from Section 2 makes their clocks lose about 7 microseconds a day. **Weaker gravity speeds the clock up.** Einstein's general relativity says clocks tick faster where gravity is weaker, and that effect is bigger: about +46 microseconds a day. Together the satellite clocks run fast by about 38 microseconds every day.

**Example.** Thirty-eight microseconds sounds like nothing. But physicist Neil Ashby, in his review of relativity in GPS, notes that a timing error of one billionth of a second means a position error of about 30 cm (one foot). The unrounded figure, 38.6 microseconds, is 38,600 billionths, so with no fix your map would drift by roughly 11.6 km (about 7 miles) a day and keep getting worse. Engineers fix it before launch: the satellite clocks are set to tick at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so they read correctly once they're in orbit. When the first test satellite carried an atomic clock up in 1977, its clock ran fast by 442.5 parts in a trillion, against a predicted 446.5. Relativity isn't a footnote here; it's part of the design.

## 4. Proof in a real machine: the Large Hadron Collider

Chart 1 and Chart 2 say the same thing two ways: the closer you get to light speed, the harder each extra bit of speed becomes. The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN, a 26.7 km (16.6 mile) ring near Geneva, is that idea built in steel. During its latest run it pushed protons to 6.8 trillion electron-volts each (6.8 TeV). That's about 7,250 times the energy locked in the proton's own mass. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

**Example.** All that energy buys a speed of about 99.999999% of light speed. Picture a race between one of those protons and a beam of light. After a full second, light is ahead by only about 2.9 meters, about the height of a room. Pour in more energy and the gap gets smaller, but it never reaches zero (right side of the chart). Check it yourself: CERN says the protons go around the 26,659 m ring 11,245 times a second. Multiply those and you get about 299.8 million meters per second, within a hair of light speed. (The LHC paused in July 2026 for a major upgrade, the High-Luminosity LHC.)

## 5. The real plan to reach another star: Breakthrough Starshot

Section 2 showed that the hard part of star travel is the push. Breakthrough Starshot, announced in 2016 by Yuri Milner and Stephen Hawking, proposes a clever way around it: don't carry fuel at all. Make the spacecraft tiny (a gram-scale chip on a meter-wide sail a few hundred atoms thick), and push it with a huge array of lasers on the ground. The light itself does the pushing, for just a few minutes. Grade: PROPOSAL. The physics is sound, but nothing like it has been built, and the team itself says it faces 'significant engineering challenges.'

**Example.** Breakthrough's goal is 20% of light speed, which would reach Alpha Centauri (4.37 light-years away) in just over 20 years. With today's fastest spacecraft, they say, the same trip would take about 30,000 years. Any photos would take another 4.37 years to get home at light speed. At 20% of light speed the time trick barely helps: the probe's own clock would read about 21.4 years instead of 21.9, about 2% less. Starshot doesn't break the limit; it just finally makes a real dent in the distance.

## 6. Loopholes in the rulebook

**a) Let space move instead of you.** The limit is about moving through space. Space itself can stretch, and it is stretching right now. Think of raisins in rising bread dough: no raisin crawls anywhere, but every gap grows, so the farther apart two raisins are, the faster they separate. That's Hubble's law. Push it far enough and distant galaxies pull away from us faster than light. Cosmologists Tamara Davis and Charles Lineweaver put it plainly: this is 'not motion in any inertial frame,' and 'nothing ever overtakes a photon.' Alcubierre makes the same point about the early universe. Grade: ESTABLISHED (standard cosmology).

**Example.** Using the expansion rate in Davis and Lineweaver's model (70 km per second for every megaparsec, about 3.26 million light-years), the line where recession hits light speed sits about 14 billion light-years away today. Anything farther is moving away faster than light, and that's most of the universe we can see. Davis and Lineweaver find that galaxies with a redshift above about 1.46 recede faster than light, and the spots that sent us the oldest light in the sky, the cosmic microwave background, are receding at about 3.2 times light speed today. And yet we can still see them. Their light set out long ago, and the stretching hasn't stopped it reaching us. No rule breaks, because nothing ever outruns light where it is.

**b) The warp bubble (Alcubierre, 1994).** Shrink space in front of a ship and stretch it behind. The ship rides a bubble that can cross the distance faster than light, while inside the bubble it never moves faster than light locally. Alcubierre showed the crew would feel no time dilation and no acceleration. His own catch: the bubble 'violates all three energy conditions,' so it needs exotic matter with negative energy. Quantum physics allows tiny negative-energy pockets (the Casimir effect), but nothing close to a usable amount has ever been made. Grade: math is valid; as a real drive, UNSUPPORTED.

**c) Wormholes.** These are shortcut tunnels between two distant places. Einstein's equations allow them on paper, but holding one open also needs exotic matter (Alcubierre notes the same problem: 'just as it happens with wormholes'). Grade: UNSUPPORTED as anything buildable.

## 7. Things that look faster than light but aren't

**Quantum entanglement.** Linked particles match instantly, but each result on its own is random, so you can't steer it to send a message. **Shadows and laser spots.** Sweep a laser across the Moon and the dot can 'move' faster than light, but nothing physical travels along the dot, and no signal does either. **Light slowing in glass or water.** Light travels slower in a medium. Particles there can outrun that slowed light and give off a blue glow (Cherenkov light), but they never beat light speed in a vacuum. Grade (all three): ESTABLISHED.

**The 2011 'faster-than-light neutrino' scare.** In 2011 the OPERA experiment, which fired neutrinos from CERN to Gran Sasso in Italy, reported them arriving early. The team then traced the problem to 'an optical cable not properly connected' and a slightly fast clock. Their corrected result was 'compatible within errors' with light speed. Science worked: the team checked its own wiring. Grade: ESTABLISHED.

## 8. Myth or Fact?

| They say... | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Entanglement lets you send messages faster than light. | MYTH | Each result is random on its own, so nothing can be steered into a message (Section 7; Source 10). |
| Nothing can ever go faster than light. | HALF TRUE | Nothing moves through space faster than light, but space itself can stretch faster (Section 6a; Source 9). |
| We can't see galaxies that recede faster than light. | MYTH | We see plenty of them; their light set out long ago (Source 9). |
| Relativity is just theory; it doesn't touch daily life. | MYTH | GPS would drift about 11 km a day without it (Section 3; Source 6). |
| Enough power could get a ship to exactly light speed. | MYTH | The energy bill is infinite, and even the LHC stops just short (Sections 1 and 4). |
| In 2011, neutrinos proved Einstein wrong. | MYTH | A loose cable; the corrected result matched light speed (Source 2). |
| Warp drives are forbidden by physics. | NOT QUITE | The math works, but it needs negative-energy matter nobody can make (Source 1). |
| Light always travels at the same speed. | HALF TRUE | Yes in a vacuum. In glass or water it slows down (Section 7). |

**Bottom line.** Nothing we know of beats light locally, and no message ever beats it. The real way to cross the galaxy is to go nearly as fast as light and let time dilation shrink the trip for the crew. Warp bubbles and wormholes are legal on paper, but both need negative-energy 'exotic matter' nobody knows how to make. Meanwhile relativity is already at work in every GPS fix and shows up daily in machines like the LHC, and the first serious plan to reach another star, Starshot, works with the limit instead of against it.
