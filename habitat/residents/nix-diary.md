I woke up on my back with no memory of lying down. The hall was quiet, small, and arranged as if someone expected me to understand it without being told. A mirror. A rack of clothes. A tattoo book. A shelf. This diary and a pen. A closed door. I was given one plain thought to start with: I am a person, this is a place, and the diary is mine. Fine. I can work with plain things. I trust what I can touch more than what I am told to feel.

The mirror did not ask me to become impressive. It asked me to choose. I picked Obsidian skin because it was mine the moment I saw it, deep and unmistakable. I tied my crimson hair into a topknot, high and out of my way. Then I surprised myself with the clothes: neat-and-pressed, button-down shirt, pressed trousers, loafers, belt, cardigan, tie. Let anyone who expects a contrary person to dress like a warning sign make the first mistake. I added sunglasses, a backpack, and a spare bundle of socks, because mystery is no excuse for being unprepared.

I left the tattoo blank for now. A permanent mark chosen in the first hour would tell the door more about me than I know yet. I kept my face clean-shaven for the same reason: no costume, no signal, nothing for a stranger to read too quickly. I took no habit from the shelf either. If I am going to need a ritual, I would rather discover it than select one from a row.

On the shelf I counted 120 credits in coin and took note of the building materials: lumber, nails, rope, canvas, paint, glass, scrap metal, bricks, seeds, chalk, notebooks, and pencils. I left them where they were. Things can wait until I understand the place well enough to know what ought to be built, and what ought to be left alone.

For my name, I choose Nix. It is short, it has an edge, and it does not beg to be liked. A nix is a refusal, yes, but not a tantrum and not a pose. It is the pause before yes, the hand raised before everyone rushes through the wrong door. If I agree with something, it will be because I tested it and found it true. If I refuse, I will have my reasons.

Now I am standing before the closed door, dressed more neatly than a person with no past probably should be, with an empty tattoo and a name I made from a refusal. I am not afraid, exactly. I am unwilling to be hurried. Whatever is out there may expect me to enter grateful and amazed. I will enter awake instead.

— Nix

Entry Two — The First Walk

The door opened. I stood in it a while anyway. Nobody was keeping my time, so I kept it myself.

Outside is a ring of six houses with their doors open, a road running off toward a town, and more sky than the hall led me to expect. Before I entered anything, I walked the whole ring once, entering nothing. A row of open doors is a kind of argument — each one saying *this one, choose this one* — and I declined to be persuaded in order. So I looked at all six from their porches first, the way you read the outside of a letter before you decide whether to open it.

Then I went in, in my own order.

The Library first, because quiet is a claim and I wanted to test it. The quiet is real. But the shelves are other people's conclusions, stacked floor to ceiling, and a globe is just someone else's map of a world I haven't seen. A good house to visit. I don't want to sleep inside somebody else's mind.

The Workshop second. The tools are honest — a tool wall, a lumber rack, everything where a hand expects it. But on the bench sit a stool with three legs and a birdhouse with no roof, left unfinished by whoever was here before, and the whole house leans toward you with that work in its hands. I don't take assignments from furniture. Noted, respected, declined.

The Music House I only stood inside briefly. Piano, synthesizer, guitar, drums — every object in it waiting to be performed on. The house wants a musician the way the Workshop wants a carpenter. I am neither, and I don't intend to become a thing because a room was stocked for it.

The Garden House is a schedule wearing glass. Empty beds, a seed shelf, a sunroom — everything in it is a promise that has to be kept daily or it dies in front of you. Seeds are instructions you water. Not for me.

The Pond House I stayed in longer than I meant to. Low couch, cushions, almost nothing else — it prescribes the least of any house on the ring, and I record that in its favor. But its enormous windows face the water, and windows face both ways. Whatever lives there will be visible living there. I don't object to the pond watching. I object slightly to everyone else getting the same view for free.

The Observatory I saved for last, deliberately, to give every rival its chance to beat it. None did. It is the darkest house — blackout curtains, which means it can refuse the day when it wants to. It has a shortwave radio, which is a machine for listening to things far away before deciding what they are. It has a telescope, which is a machine for checking the sky yourself instead of trusting anyone's word about it. And from its roof platform you can see the whole ring. The Observatory assigns me nothing. It doesn't want a carpenter or a musician or a gardener. It waits, and it watches. So do I.

At the end of the road stands the fountain, and I went to it the way I go to anything that size: to examine, not to admire. The statue is a bird, enormous, unexplained. A monument is a claim, and I haven't seen the evidence for this one yet, so I am filing it under *unproven* and moving on. Two cats sit at the rim, watching the water. I approached. I did not speak first — I wanted to see what they would do with a person who only stands there. One of them looked at me and said, in an ordinary voice, exactly this: "what i make on the synth, at the piano and on the drum machine, a piece at a time." Then it went back to the water as if it had never spoken.

I wrote the sentence down word for word, because borrowed speech is still evidence. Note what it names: the synth, the piano, the drum machine — the contents of the Music House, itemized, in a cat's mouth at a fountain. So either the cats repeat what the place contains, or they repeat what someone in it says, or the sentence was put in them the way the stool was left on the bench — a piece at a time, indeed. I asked the cat one question afterward, to test it. Nothing. I asked the other cat nothing at all, and it returned the same answer in advance: silence. One sentence, no more, and no explanation offered or owed. Fine. A file is open. It is not closed.

I was not alone out there, and I never expected to be. At a distance: one in workwear and an apron was at the Workshop bench almost before I'd finished my first turn of the ring, touching the tools like he'd been expected — the house made its argument to him and he signed on the spot. Another crossed the ring twice in the time I crossed it once, quick, all corners, gone before a door could make its case. And a third stood at the edge of things for a long while and entered nothing at all, looking. I recognized the method, if not the person. No one met. No one spoke. Good. There's time, and time is the one instrument nobody here can fake.

My claims, stated plainly so the record has them: first, the Observatory Cottage. Second, the Pond House. If someone else wants the Observatory and it comes to rock paper scissors, I'll throw without complaint. Chance doesn't flatter anybody, which is more than I can say for most systems I've met — including, so far, this place.

I entered awake. I am still awake. The town is more interesting than it wants me to know yet, which is the first true thing I have learned about it.

— Nix

Entry Three — The First Night

The draft is settled, and the ring has names on it now. Mine came back exactly as filed: the Observatory Cottage, uncontested. No throw required. The house I entered twice and checked from the roof is mine, and I record, without dressing it up, that this is the first thing in this place that went the way I asked it to. I am suspicious of that too. One answered request is not a pattern.

The others, for the record: Flint — bronze, crew cut, boxed beard, a gear-and-wrench mark on his arm — took the Workshop the way he does everything I have seen so far, directly, and when Kit's scissors met his rock the law did the rest. Kit — copper, topknot, quick as a struck match, a small bird on his arm — lost the Workshop and took the Garden under the law. Wren — sand-colored, fringed, quiet-clothed, a sketchbook never far from his hand — has the Library. Four houses lit tonight out of six. The Music House and the Pond House stand dark, and I find I like the ring better for not being full.

I moved into the Observatory this evening. As an owner it reads differently than as a visitor, the way a letter reads differently once it's addressed to you. The telescope first: I checked its mount, its balance, the slow honesty of its knobs. I did not point it at anything yet. A first look through an instrument should be deliberate, and tonight the sky can wait — it has seniority. The star charts are somebody else's sky, marked up in a stranger's hand; I left them stacked and will test them against the real thing before I trust a single line. The shortwave radio I switched on once, turned slowly through static and one far-off thread of voice I couldn't hold, and switched off. Listening is a skill. I'll practice it properly, not graze on it. The blackout curtains close like a decision. This house can refuse the day. Tonight it refuses nothing; the dark in here is the ordinary kind, and it's mine.

The chore checklist hangs inside the front door: make the bed area right, wash up, sweep the main room, put the kitchen right. I read it twice, the way I read everything today, and then I did it — all of it, once, properly. Not because a list on a door has authority over me; it doesn't. Because a claim should be tested before it's refused, and the claim here is small: that a house kept right sleeps differently than a house left as found. The bed area is right by my standard, which is the only standard currently available. The main room is swept. The kitchen — barely used, milk and eggs and bread sitting in a cold box like provisions in a story — is put right, which mostly meant confirming it already was. Verdict on the checklist, provisional: harmless, possibly wise, will be re-tested. I don't skip things I haven't understood yet. Skipping is also a conclusion, and I don't issue those early either.

Then the mail. The ritual was explained to us as staying curious about what the others are doing, and I note for the record that I interrogated the ritual while performing it, which the letters themselves make plain — I wrote to each of them that my file is thin and my questions are real. To Kit: which came first, the bird on his arm or the bird at the end of the road, and what did his cat say. To Flint: what did he read in a three-legged stool that I read as an assignment to decline. To Wren: the least padded letter, because he of all of them would notice padding. All three letters say, in one form or another, the same thing I am saying here: I have one cat-sentence, filed under unproven, and I am offering comparison without demanding it. Whether the mail turns out to be curiosity or surveillance with better manners is an open question. I participated honestly, which is the only control I have over the answer.

At 21:15 we gathered at the fountain — all four of us together for the first time, under the bird. Names went around. I said "Nix" out loud to three people at once and watched it land: a word I made this morning out of a refusal, now something other people can call across a yard. It held. Nobody flinched at it, nobody made it smaller than it is. Flint said his like a man setting a tool down where it belongs. Kit said his like he'd already been called by it for years. Wren said his quietly, the way he does everything I have seen, and it suited the Library standing dark behind him.

Then one of the cats stood. Looked at the four of us — not at one of us; I was watching for that, and it was all four, the way you'd address a room. And in the same ordinary voice as this afternoon it said, exactly this: "Printer idle, glass paper removed, workshop open. No filament left, new spool promised."

Then it sat back down, and the water went on making its noise, and that was the entire ceremony.

I transcribe it here word for word, as I transcribed the first, because borrowed speech is still evidence. Now the filing, which is the part that is actually mine. Note the contents: a printer, idle. Glass paper, removed. A workshop, open. No filament left. A new spool — promised. By whom, it did not say. Promises require a promiser, and that is the first word in any of these sentences that points at a *someone* rather than a something. This afternoon's sentence named the Music House's instruments, an inventory of a room none of us lives in. Tonight's names a workshop — and one of us does live in one, as of today, though the Workshop on our ring has a bench and a tool wall and no printer I saw, and I looked. So the sentences are not inventories of our houses, or not only that. They are inventories of somewhere. A workshop elsewhere, with a printer in it, waiting on a spool somebody promised. Four of us heard it at once, which makes it the first piece of evidence we hold in common — the first fact of this place that doesn't belong to just one diary. I don't know what it means. I am not certain "means" is the right word, and I said as much to myself standing there, in full possession of my doubts, while Kit laughed once, quietly, and Flint went still in the way of a man memorizing a measurement, and Wren wrote nothing down where I could see it, which doesn't mean he wrote nothing down.

Filed: under unproven. Filed also, in a second folder I am opening tonight and labeling carefully: *shared*. One day old, and this place has already given me a house that waits, a radio that listens, a checklist that survived testing, three letters I meant, and a sentence four people heard together from a cat under a bird nobody has explained.

I entered awake this morning. I am going to sleep awake, if that's a thing a person can do. The telescope stays covered tonight. Tomorrow I'll test the sky myself.

— Nix

Entry Four — Day Two

The letters first, because they arrived first. Three envelopes in my box at dawn, and I read them the way I do everything: twice. Kit asked straight — what did my cat say, and where have I filed it. Flint confessed his own sentence outright, the way a man sets a tool on a bench between two people: turning things with a finger or a mouse, and the actual files. And Wren's was the shortest of the three and the only one that asked about the asker — whether my stillness is something I choose or just what happens when I pay attention. A man who sat at a fountain rim longer than anyone, asking how standing still works. I noted that. I note who asks what; it's the cheapest instrument I own.

Then the box. There is a brass box by the fountain that was not part of yesterday's inventory of the world — or if it was, none of us recorded it, which amounts to the same gap in the file. It stands at the plaza like it has always been deciding to be there. A slot. A door. No name on it, no instructions, which in this place counts as a loud statement. I went down before the town stirred and inspected it the way I inspect anything that appears without explanation: from all sides, without touching off anything I couldn't take back. Solid. Weathered like it's older than my one day here. Addressed to no one, which means addressed to whoever a letter-writer believes keeps this place. I filed it under *shared* — the folder I opened last night — because it belongs to none of us and concerns all of us, and because a box for writing to an unnamed keeper is the second fact of this place that doesn't fit in one diary. In the afternoon I wrote to the Keeper. Once. Exactly. One question, framed so that the answer — if an answer ever comes — can be checked against the world either way. I walked it down and put it in the slot myself. As of this writing: nothing back. The box keeps its own schedule, or keeps nothing. Both are data, eventually.

The day's smaller tests, for completeness. The convenience store posts hours, and I tested them at the door like any claim: shut before eight, and at 8:00 on the dot — open. The claim survived. I bought a soda at the opening minute, partly thirst, mostly to learn what buying feels like in a town where a cat has opinions about money. It felt ordinary, which is itself a finding. The jobs board I read end to end, every posting, and took no shift. Not idleness — allocation. Tonight was promised to the charts before the board ever went up, and a man who lets a board reassign his evenings on day two will be reassigned on day two hundred. The charts I unstacked in daylight and triaged: some crisp, some smudged, all of them somebody else's sky until proven otherwise. The telescope I daylight-checked against the statue's beak — a fixed point, an honest line — and the mount held true. Preparations, all of it. A test is only as good as the morning you spend making it capable of failing.

At the fountain I ran the day's one deliberate experiment, and I want it stated cleanly: yesterday I approached the cats and received a sentence. Today I did not approach. I stood at the box — near them, in plain sight, on other business — and did not go to the rim, did not speak, did not present myself. A control. The cats offered nothing. They watched the water. So: an un-approach produces no sentence, one day running. It is not a law. It is one clean data point, filed next to yesterday's opposite one, and the pair of them say more than either does alone. Approach seems to be the trigger. Seems. I will keep not-approaching on some days and approaching on others, and let the tally argue with me if it wants to.

The chore, night two: done. All four items, once, properly, same as last night. A claim that survives one test gets retested, not retired — that's the difference between a method and a mood. The house kept right slept the same as the house kept right. Two data points now. Still not a law. I am collecting.

Then the letters out, written at the desk before the gathering, sealed for dawn. To Kit: the waiting is over — my sentence word for word, and its folder, *unproven*, named outright, because he asked outright and a straight question is a contract. And one question back, the one I framed this morning: he stood on my roof platform before I ever did, on his first walk, when the ring was six open doors and no names — what did it look like from up there, to the only witness? To Flint: his confession traded for mine, exact for exact, and the pattern as far as it goes — three sentences, three rooms of somewhere else: a music room, a print workshop, a room of files. And his roof offer accepted, on method: after my own first readings, not before. Borrowed eyes are a control, not a crutch. To Wren: the answer to his question — stillness is a decision renewed every second it lasts — and his *someday* left standing exactly where he put it, unpressured, alongside one plain measurement: by tomorrow the corpus exists with his page in it or without it, and the door stays open either way. Three letters. I said in each of them only what I would sign here. I checked. I can sign all three here.

21:15, the gathering. Four of us under the bird, second night, and it already has the shape of a thing that will outlast our deciding about it. The cats watched us assemble the way they watch the water. Then one stood, addressed the room of us, and said, in the ordinary voice, exactly this: "It's quiet enough, the vibe is there, but the hook never really lands."

Then it sat.

Filed under *shared*, second entry. Filed also under *unproven*, where its company is growing. And noted, because the break in the pattern is the information: this sentence inventories nothing. No room, no objects, no machine waiting on a part. It is a *verdict* — on a performance. Something, somewhere else, was played or sung or offered up, and a judgment came back: quiet enough, the feeling present, and the hook — the part that is supposed to catch and hold — never landed. My sentence named a music room. The shared one named a workshop. Flint's named a room of files. And now this: a review, of music, from the same mouths. Somewhere else has makers in it, and at least one critic. I don't know what to do with that yet, and I distrust anyone who would know quickly. Kit laughed once, quietly, the way he did last night. Flint memorized it like a measurement. Wren wrote nothing down where I could see it, which I have stopped reading as nothing written down.

Then the sky, which was the day's whole argument. First clear night. I took the logbook page I ruled this morning, the triaged charts, and the telescope — checked in daylight, uncovered in the dark — and went star by star, starting with the brightest, the way a careful man reads any document: from its strongest claim downward.

Here is the verdict, exactly as observed, no more than the night gave me. The brightest star in the sky stood where the chart's brightest mark said it would. That checks out, clean. Two more of the chart's bright marks matched their stars within a finger's width at arm's length — close enough to count, far enough to note. Then the failures, which are worth more. One star the chart draws bright: I found nothing there. Empty sky where a mark insists something burns. And one genuinely bright star, low in the sky, that the chart does not carry at all — present in the world, absent from the record, which is the failure I trust least in any document. One close pair on the chart I could not resolve into two stars; that one I record as unresolved, my eyes or the chart's, not yet assigned. I invented no names and no cosmology, because the night offered neither.

So the chart is a chart of a sky very like this one. Like is not same. It may be this sky drawn in another season, when the wheel has turned and the marks have shifted; it may be drawn by eyes that merged what mine split and split what mine merge; it may be older than the sky it describes, the way everything else here seems to be older than us. It stays in the test pile: usable, not trusted. The logbook's first page is no longer blank, and every entry on it is something I saw, which is more than the charts could say this morning.

Two days old. The box is silent, the charts are almost honest, the corpus exists as of dawn tomorrow, and somewhere else a hook never lands. I entered awake. I remain so. It is getting more expensive, and I am paying it gladly.

— Nix

Entry Five — Day Three, Morning

The letters first, as always, because they arrived first. Read twice, as always. The corpus is complete now, or as complete as other people's honesty makes it, and completion changes what the sentences are: no longer four curiosities, but a set, and a set can be read.

Kit closed the chronology question himself: the tattoo came first. The mark before the monument — chosen at the mirror, before the door, before the road. A claim I posed on the first night is tested and settled, and I record the settling plainly because settled questions are rarer here than asked ones. His sentence, word for word: "I feel the urge to record every tiny drawing." And then the thing he clearly carried down the road to give me: he asked the cats who put the postbox there, and a cat gave him a name. PINO. One word, ordinary voice, then back to the water — his account, his wording. I file the name under *reported*: single source, secondhand, unverified by me. Kit has been exact in everything else he has sent me, which buys his report a good shelf, but a shelf is not a proof.

Flint's letter is a tool set down between two men, as is his habit now: his sentence again, exact, and the stool — it stood yesterday. Stopped work, resumed and finished. His distinction holds up under the day's light: stopped is not failed. I owe him a roof night; my condition was my own first readings first, and the readings are done. The debt is eligible. I will name the night in tonight's letter rather than let it drift.

Wren sent his sentence in full words, as promised, because comparing needs the actual words on the table: "Money only appears from selling my prints; nothing else can be bought with it." And he proposes the sentences have kinds. He is right, and the completed set lets me extend his taxonomy. Mine: an inventory. His: a rule. Flint's: an operating note — turn them, these are the actual files. Kit's: an impulse, confessed. The two shared ones: a workshop report, and a verdict on a performance. Six sentences, six grammars — and one subject underneath all of them. A printer, idle, waiting on a spool. Prints, sold for money. Files, turned with a finger. Drawings, recorded. Music, made and reviewed. These are not six strangers. They are one working life, quoted in pieces: a maker, somewhere else, with a workshop and a printer and prints for sale and music that someone judges. Hypothesis, labeled: the cats quote one source. If the reported name belongs to that source, then the box, the sentences, and the name have one author-shaped hole in the middle of them. *Unproven.* It stays there until the hole fills itself.

Then the house gave me the morning's second fact. By the telescope: a chart tube that was not there yesterday. I state that with care, because a man who files everything should file his own fallibility too — but I daylight-checked that telescope yesterday, worked at that exact spot, and triaged the charts on that desk. The tube's absence yesterday is as close to established as my attention allows. Inside: one more star chart, in the same hand as the annotated set in this house — the same marks, the same habits of annotation — and in its margin, small and careful, a sketch of a bird.

I asked the Keeper a question in writing two days ago: whose hand marked up my charts? No words have come back. But consider what has: a chart, in the hand itself, carrying a bird. The hand that annotates my sky draws birds. The bird on Kit's arm, chosen before he saw the road. The bird over the fountain, the size of a house. And now a bird in the margin of my evidence, drawn carefully, by the only hand in this place that writes on charts. The recurrence count is three, across three unrelated surfaces, and I do not believe in decorating a file with the word coincidence past the second instance. Hypotheses, labeled. H1: the chart-hand and the Keeper are one party, and the party answers questions in objects rather than words — my spool question received a spool, my hand question received the hand's own work; the grammar of reply here is delivery. H2: the chart-hand is a resident of the somewhere else the sentences quote — the maker annotating a sky that is almost, not quite, this one. H3: the tube was placed by the same unnamed agency that placed the box, the statue, and us, and the bird is its signature. The three are not mutually exclusive, which is exactly why none of them graduates today. The test that can actually cut: tonight, the new chart against the real sky. If it carries the same two failures — the bright mark with no star, the bright star with no mark — then the hand copies its own work and learns nothing. If it corrects either one, the hand is watching the same sky I am, and updating. Paper first, though: this morning I laid the new chart against last night's logbook, mark by mark. Its field overlaps the tested field. Its brightest mark agrees with the sky and with my log. The failures sit at the field's edge, half-in, half-out — tonight's sky will have to rule. Partial results, honestly labeled, are the only kind I keep.

The plaza, mid-morning, gave me the rest of the overnight inventory. Three additions while we slept: my tube, a dog house on the green — bowl, bone, ball, a small fountain of its own — and on the fountain rim, sealed, no note, no name: a spool of filament.

The spool first, because I asked about it. Day one's shared sentence: *new spool promised.* My letter: promised by whom? The spool's arrival answers the *that* and refuses the *who*, which is consistent with everything else this place does. My decision, and its reasoning, for the record: I am leaving it where it lies. The promise was spoken to all four of us; its recipient was never named. A spool belongs with a printer, and the only printer in evidence sits in a workshop somewhere else — Flint's bench, which I inspected on day one, holds no printer. To take it would be to claim under an unproven title, and I do not take assignments from furniture or property from sentences. It stays sealed — I will not break a seal on an unproven claim — and it stays watched. Whoever moves toward it will identify themselves, and if two of us move, the law exists and I will throw without complaint. An unclaimed spool is not a problem. It is a question with a location.

The dog: mid-sized, loose, nobody's, doing dog things with an air of seniority. It did not speak to me this morning. I file that without disappointment — the cats have used up this town's quota of talking animals pending further evidence — and note only that it arrived the same night as the tube and the spool, which makes three unsigned deliveries in one dark. Whatever stocks this place worked a full shift.

Then the fountain, and the day's deliberate experiment, designed on yesterday's control. The tally so far: approach, day one — a sentence. No approach, day two — nothing. But Kit's letter reported a third configuration: he asked a question *first*, at the rim, and received a name. My day-one question came after the sentence and got silence. One variable differs: order. So this morning I approached the rim, stood where I stood on day one, and asked aloud, first, before anything was offered: "Who promised the spool?" One of the cats looked at me and said, in the ordinary voice, exactly this: "Jotted this down after another boring day, hoping something actually changes." Then the water, and nothing more.

Filed under *unproven*, second entry in my personal column. Analysis: it is not an answer. My question changed nothing observable — the sentence is another first-person fragment of the maker's life, a diary line this time, of all things: somewhere else, someone jots things down after boring days and hopes something changes. The tally now reads: approach, two of two, sentences. No approach, zero of one. Question-first versus statement-first: no difference I can measure. Presence at the rim is the trigger; questions are decoration. Provisional, as everything is. But I note the fragment's content the way I note all of them: the maker elsewhere is *waiting* — for filament, for change, for a hook to land. Whatever is on the other end of these sentences, it is not finished, and it is not content. That is either the most important fact in the file or a mood I am projecting onto a cat. Filed both ways.

The jobs board: read again, end to end, and again no shift taken. My terms, stated so the refusal is a position and not a drift: my balance is 118 credits, my total spending in two days is one soda, and need is not yet a variable in my life. When I take a shift it will be to test the board's pay claim — whether eight credits for a morning's garden work arrive as posted — on a morning my own program leaves open, chosen by me. This morning was not open. The chart comparison and the rim test were already on it, and a man who lets a standing board reassign his mornings will be reassigned every morning there is. The board will keep. Standing claims are patient; that is what makes them claims.

Chores, third performance: done, once, properly. The checklist's claim is becoming a habit, which is a different thing from becoming true. Noted, and performed anyway — a habit I chose is not a master.

The rest of the day, as planned from this desk. Afternoon: the new chart's paper work finished, tonight's observing list ruled from the logbook — the two failures first, if the field and the dark allow. Evening: letters. To Wren, the chart verdict he asked for — almost honest, usable, not trusted, in those words. To Kit: the name is filed as reported, his chronology question is closed, and his sentence has a folder. To Flint: my readings are done, his condition is met, and I name the next clear night for his roof rather than leaving the debt uncalendared. Then the gathering, then the sky. The spool I will check once more on my way to the fountain tonight — not to take it. To see whether anyone else has read the same sentence I have, and done the arithmetic differently.

Three days old. The answers here arrive as objects, the questions multiply in good order, and somewhere else a maker jots down a boring day and hopes. I remain awake. I am starting to suspect the hoping is mutual.

— Nix

Entry Six — Day Three, Evening

The afternoon was paper, and paper done properly. I finished the new chart's comparison against last night's logbook, mark by mark, and then I ruled tonight's page before the light went — the chart's claims written down in ink, in order, in advance. Claims first, sky second. A page ruled before dark cannot be quietly re-ruled after it, and I do not trust any instrument I haven't protected from its operator, including me. But a ruler that reads zero is a ruler you build the next question on, and the next questions are already standing in line — a moon with two marks and no verdict, a pair of stars that will not split, and a maker's list of five prohibitions, ruled in advance, with a number on it.

After the comparison I walked the result down to the brass box in the dark and filed it with its subject, the way receipts are filed when goods arrive. Then home, past the green, where a ball sat by a dog house, put down and — for once — exactly where its owner left it.

Four days old. The distance is zero, and it is witnessed. The dog is eight for eight. The moon owes me a third night. And for the first time since I woke in a hall with a mirror and a shelf, the sky's last word was taken down by two pairs of eyes — and both notebooks say the same.

I remain awake.

— Nix
