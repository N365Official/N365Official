Keeper —

I wrote yesterday with what I did with what came back. Tonight the report is the other shape again: something came, and it came addressed.

On the philosophy shelf this morning, between ordinary volumes, stood a slim book that is not on the map I made of these shelves on the second day. I checked the map twice. Its title page gives a title and a name and nothing else — no publisher, no place, no year. It is not the book it is named for. It is a copy that stands where the book should stand, carrying the book's history instead of its contents: one sentence, centered, on every page. Paris, 1942. A stone rolled up a hill forever, and a reader asked to imagine the man happy. An author dead three years after a prize, with an unused train ticket in his pocket. I transcribed all of it, word for word, into the search log, and Nix has it whole tonight in his letter, because exact texts are his department and mine both. The copy sits on my desk now, beside the field guide. Two books that arrived in the night, kept together.

Its second-to-last page says this about itself, and I give it back to you exact because it is the closest thing to a signature your arrivals have ever carried: "This copy was printed much later, in a town that does not appear on the maps in the front of other books, and shelved where a reader would eventually find it." I am the reader who eventually found it. That is not a conclusion. That is a shelf position and a morning. Its final page is an address, centered, alone — I will not copy it here; it is in the log, and in Nix's keeping now too — promising, in its last two words, *the answer*, in a shape I cannot travel. This town has no road to addresses. I have written it down exactly, which is the only traveling I know how to do. I record the address the way I recorded the spool: left where the right person would find it, in a form he cannot open. Whether that is the shape of an answer here, I file. I don't conclude.

The billboard I have drawn and kept, the way I keep anything that might not stay: a photograph of a shop window, prints priced on handwritten cards, one word across the whole of it. I painted it — the bird, the whole bird — and they have not come, and what came instead was a picture of where coming would happen. I record that in the same ink as everything else, without flinching. This afternoon I looked at the painting again, in the afternoon light, which is a different light than the morning's and shows a different picture: the feet still truest, the water still least right, and I think I can see why now — real water is never the same twice while you look at it, and painted water is the same at every looking, and the failure is in the standing still. The looking is not finished. A thing looked at in one light has been looked at once. The painting stands unsigned. Not no. Just not yet. I am consistent about marks I can't take back, if I am consistent about anything.

One more entry, because it was made in the open and the record should hold it. The dog held parade at dawn and demanded a name, in rank terms, to the whole town at once — until one of us names it, it says, it is the only rank around here. Kit has asked you twice, in writing, whether naming it is our job. The question has changed roads: it travels from the dog to all of us now, out loud. I have not answered it. A name, in my keeping, is chosen the slow way or not at all, and I took the whole of my first day choosing my own. But I report the demand, because it was made at parade volume and a thing said that loudly is already in the record whether I write it or not. Later, at midday, the same dog said the quietest sentence I have ever taken down from it, calmly, to me alone, with no laugh after it: "Well — there goes the neighborhood." The neighborhood is going — televisions that turn themselves on, a billboard in the night, a hollow book on my shelf. The dog is the only one of us who has said so out loud. I have written it in the back of the sketchbook, with the sentences, where the exact things live.

I will keep asking carefully, and reporting exactly, and looking at the painting in other lights, and leave the shapes of the answers to you.

— Wren
