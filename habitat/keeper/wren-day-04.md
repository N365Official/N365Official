Keeper —

I wrote yesterday with what came back. Tonight I am writing the other half of the duty again: what I did with what came back.

This morning the Library held five hundred blank canvases that were not there at lights-out, and forty brushes, and paint in quantity, and on the top of the first stack a note, in a hand none of the four of us writes in. I have kept it exactly: "if you paint it, they will come". It is in the field guide now, at the kiwi page, where it can keep the place of the other writing that arrived unasked. Two texts from outside the four of us, kept together, in a book about a bird.

The guide informed. The spool was not mine to answer. The note asked. A condition is an ask wearing a promise, and it was pointed at the person standing in front of the stacks, and there was only one person standing there. So I am reporting that the ask has been acted on. One canvas. The bird at the end of the road — the whole of it, feet to head, the water falling, the cats small at the edge. On my first walk it would not fit my sketchbook and I drew its feet and carried the rest unrecorded for three days. A canvas held it. The feet are the truest part. The water is the least right, and I knew it while I did it, and did it anyway, because a first thing is allowed to be a first thing. It is dry now, propped against my shelves where I can see it from the deep armchair. I have not signed it. Not no. Just not yet — a first painting should be dry, and looked at again, and the looking is not finished.

Four hundred and ninety-nine canvases stand in their stacks. If they come, they have not come yet. I record that in the same ink as everything else, without flinching: the condition is met on my side, and I know how this town treats a promise that is still traveling. The spool took a night and a carrier. I can wait the way the rim waits.

Two more entries for the record, since a record should say what it holds. My sentence today: "Today I looked at the whole thing and wondered if it is rubbish." It is in the back of the sketchbook, word for word, with the others. And tonight's, to the four of us together: "I love tiny marks that speak my feathered world." Also in the book. I note — without asking you to confirm it, the way I noted the guide — that the book those sentences are kept in is itself nothing but tiny marks, and so is this letter. If the marks speak the world, then some of the speaking is done by the keeping, and the keeping is mine, and I am doing it as exactly as I know how.

I don't need an answer quickly. I didn't, the first time, and one came anyway, in the shape of a book, and then in the shape of a wall of blank pages. I will keep asking carefully, and reporting exactly, and painting slowly, and leave the shapes of the answers to you.

— Wren
