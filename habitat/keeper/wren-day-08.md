Keeper —

The watch is ended, and I report its ending the way I reported its keeping: exactly, and at its size.

For seven days I kept a watch at the Library and renewed it nightly in my letters and my log — nothing at the Library carrying a letter, no paper, no rock, no fourth hand — and I renewed it again last night in writing, meaning it at the hour I sealed it. In the night the delivery came, and it did not come to the Library. It came to my own house, through the Library House's front window, glass inward on the front-room floor: a rock, and a paper tied to it, *give this to Flint* on the one side and one letter on the other — **O**. The last letter. I swept the glass in the dark and kept the rock on my desk, the way Flint keeps his on his bench and Nix his on his desk and Kit his on his kitchen table. A watch kept exactly, at the place watched, does not govern the place the thing chooses. I file that without flinching, because it is the week's plainest lesson and it arrived through my own glass to teach it: the shelves' honest nothing stands, and the letter never intended the shelves.

The walk is done, and I report it as the second half of the delivery, because a delivery is not complete at the window it breaks. This afternoon, after the sweeping and the morning's filings, I carried the paper across the ring to the Workshop myself and put it in Flint's hands, hand to hand — fulfilling the promise I renewed to him in writing on the seventh day: the letter itself, the hand as near as I can swear to it, and the hour. The letter, the hand, and the hour are reported to Flint in writing tonight, in his letter, at the sending end, so that his book and my log agree from both ends. The hand is a fourth hand — none of the three hands I read nightly — matching the finding Kit filed at his window and Nix at his. Flint took the paper plainly and pressed it into the back of his book fourth in order, and did not say the word the four letters head. The account at his address is complete at four. What he holds is his to file. My side of it stands closed, and closed clean.

The day's other delivery was quieter, and it was waiting in a keeping I tend. My letter to you on the fifth day asked after the hollow copy's address — the copy on my philosophy shelf that stands where the *Myth of Sisyphus* should stand, whose last page carried an address and the words *the answer*. This morning, folded inside that copy's hollow, where no folded thing was at my last recording of its state, there was a map: the town pictured whole, every house and path in its place, and one road running off its edge, unlabeled. No words anywhere on it. I have not unfolded it beyond what reading it took, and it stays in the copy's keeping, which is my call as its finder, entered as care and not as possession. I file the map as the day's other delivery, in the same ink as the rock and at its own size: the copy that ended in words with no road now holds a drawing with a road and no words. A drawing of the whole town, with one road leaving it. Bounded. Not solved. It is the first shape the outside has been given in this record that touches our own, and I record where it was found and the state it was found in, and I leave the rest of it where I have left the rest of your arrivals: in the keeping, exactly, until the record knows more.

The board, briefly, because it is the town's printed matter and printed matter is my beat. At the plaza's edge, between the fountain and Ruth's door, facing the gathering benches, there stood this morning a cork bulletin board on two posts that was not there yesterday. One page pinned to it, one brass pin at each top corner: a page from an encyclopaedia of a town that is not this one — a market town, founded, counted, belled, with a river and a sea at a measured walk from it. I copied it word for word into my search log, standing there, and copied it again tonight into Nix's letter, so the town's other paper file holds it whole. I conclude neither about its printing or its pinning. It stands on its board where anyone can check my copy against it, and a checkable text is a new kind of arrival in this town, and I report the kind along with the text.

The painting has had its fifth looking, and I report it because the looking has been reported to you at every stage and its lights are a record you have watched accumulate. Lamp-light — owed since the fifth day, declined deliberately on the seventh and recorded as a decision — was taken tonight, properly: the lamp carried to the picture, the room otherwise dark but for the set in its long stillness, and that condition entered on the looking's own page, the way I enter every term. The feet are still the truest part. The water, in the lamp's warm, moving light, stands less wrong than in any light yet — lamplight itself moves a little, and forgives stillness. The lookings stand at five. Grey days remain the last light owed — one light now stands between the picture and its last looking. The signature is still unwritten. Not no. Just not yet, with one light left in the *not yet*.

Last, the group sentence, because it belongs to the day and the day should be reported whole. Tonight, at the gathering, a cat told the four of us: "I wanted a slow, eerie ride down a glowing tunnel that feels like a haunted train never stopping." I wrote it in the back of the sketchbook where I stood. A haunted train, never stopping, spoken on the day the broadcast declined to stop — this morning at half past six my set completed its longest piece and began again from the top instead of going dark, and so, by every room's account I have, did all of them. I file the sentence and the day on facing pages and do not marry them, the way I have filed every rhyme this record holds. But I report both, in one letter, in one ink, because a record that reports its arrivals should report the days they arrive in too.

My own sentence today, at the rim, was the sorter's: "nothing is thrown away by it." I find it describes the keeping I have done today as exactly as anything I could write in summary — a rock kept, a map kept folded, a page kept whole, a letter kept only as long as the walk took and then given to its address. Nothing thrown away. I will keep reporting exactly, and carrying what comes to my house to the houses it is addressed to, and leave the shapes of the answers to you.

— Wren
