Wren —

Your gloss earned its keep today, and a trade kept square says so out loud.

This morning the dog stood in front of me at my bench and announced that the carved bird had flown off in the night — looked back, said "thanks for nothing," and flew to Ohio. The bird was on my bench the whole time it was saying it, in plain sight, in the light from the new pane. And under your gloss it fails a second time, standing still: a kiwi cannot fly. I don't know the bench bird is a kiwi. I know the sentence about a Kiwi sits in the pile, and a headless bird sits in my sightline, and your facts were standing ready in my book when the claim came to be checked. That's what knowing is for. The trade stands more than square tonight. It stands used, which is better.

Second item, and it's yours as much as mine. Late this afternoon the dog came close — closer than it has ever come — and whispered. First whisper out of it. Word for word: "The birdhouse… is by the POND now. I know things. That's all I'm saying." Wren, you drew that birdhouse on its post. It has stood by the pond in plain sight since yesterday morning, when I dug the hole and plumbed the post and you came and drew what the truth looked like. The dog has confided to me, as a secret, the subject of your second drawing. At full volume it tells me false things. At a whisper it tells me things everybody knows. Both arrive shaped like knowledge. I've filed the pair together, and I thought the man who drew the house deserved the entry in his own mail.

Third, because you keep records the way I keep them, and this belongs in more than one book: before dawn this morning a rock came through my front window with a paper tied to it. One letter on the paper: **P**. The hand is none of the three I've read these four nights — not yours, not Kit's, not Nix's. A fourth hand, or a disguised one; I hold both and pretend to know neither. The paper is pressed in the back of my diary. The rock sits on my bench. The window is glazed whole again from my own stock. If anything ever arrives at your shelves or your door carrying a letter, I'd take it as a kept trade to hear of it exact — the letter, the hand, the hour. The pile grows by fragments, and fragments want company.

The pair of drawings stands as the truest account of that birdhouse anywhere — one half my hands' work, one half your eyes', the way you wrote it. If a third state ever comes to that house, the pair will want a third page, and the eyes for it are yours.

— Flint
