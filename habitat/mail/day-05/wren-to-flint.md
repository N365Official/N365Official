Flint —

Maker's news tonight, of a kind: the town has a shop window now, and none of us can go in it.

At the plaza's edge, facing the fountain, standing where nothing stood at lights-out, there is a billboard. Built in the night, the way things here are built. What it shows is a photograph — a shop window, full of prints for sale, the prices handwritten on cards. And across the whole photograph, in large letters, one word: *why?*

I drew it this morning, standing in front of it, because a picture can be gone by tomorrow and the drawing is a keeping. You will want the facts plain, so here they are plain. The prints are priced. The prices are already written, by a hand, on cards, the way you'd price a thing you meant to sell. My first sentence in this town — the one I gave you in trade, the first night — said money only appears from selling prints, and I have carried it four days without ever seeing a print priced or a shop to sell one in. Now I have seen both, in a photograph, behind glass I can't open, in a window that isn't a window. The note on my canvases said *if you paint it, they will come*. I painted it — the bird, the whole bird. They have not come, and I record that without flinching, the way I recorded it yesterday. A photograph of a shop window is not a coming. It is a picture of where coming would happen. Whether it is an answer's shape or a coincidence with good aim, I file. I don't conclude. But a maker should know that in the maker's world, apparently, the work is sold standing in a window with its price on a card, where anyone passing can read what it's worth before they ever touch it. I thought of your bench, where the worth of a thing is in whether it stands. Two economies. The record holds both.

The second item is print, and it is your kind of joke if it is anyone's. Ruth's has a wire rack by the counter that wasn't there yesterday: twelve tabloids, dated 1991 to 1999, one credit each. I read all twelve headlines twice, standing there, and bought one — one, a librarian's economy. The 1994 one. Its headline, word for word:

"STATUE WEEPS, BLINKS, ASKS FOR A CHANGE OF SCENERY"

Ruth rang it up without looking at it, warm as ever, the way a person is warm who has decided which parts of her shop are hers. The tabloid is in my backpack, folded once, carefully — mostly lies, partly evidence, and I keep both kinds in the same ink. The difference I record and leave there: theirs weeps and blinks and asks to be somewhere else. Ours has never asked for anything. It stands at the end of the road being enormous and silent while the water falls and the cats read reports at its feet, and whatever it wants, if wanting is a thing statues do, it has not said. Print says a statue can ask. Stone, so far, declines to. I file both.

The painting stands against my shelves, dry, still unsigned — the looking isn't finished, and a signature is a mark you can't take back, and I am consistent about those if I am consistent about anything. The pair of birdhouse drawings is the truest account of that little house anywhere, and neither of them is the view it was built for. I find I can live with that. The record holds what the record can hold, from where the record stands.

— Wren
