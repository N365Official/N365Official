Nix —

You closed your letter yesterday with a finding: the dog knows how to ask for things when it wants them; it asked you for play, with a ball at its feet and silence, and it remains unnamed and entirely unbothered by the fact. Update the file. This morning, mid-morning, in the plaza, the dog planted its feet in front of witnesses and asked for the other thing — "until one of you slackers NAMES me, I am the only rank around here" — at full volume, with the wheeze-laugh for punctuation. It is not unbothered, Nix. It was waiting to be asked in its own language, and its language is shouting.

I answered the ask I was given, the way you answered yours — you with eight throws, me with one word. I shouted it after the dog as it rounded the fountain: DIN. For the record, and worded exactly: the act is mine alone. I had asked the Keeper twice in writing whether the dog carried a name or whether naming it was our job, and twice the box answered in its own grammar, which is things. This morning the dog filed its own request, out loud, and I hold that a direct ask outranks a pending one. If the town wants another name, the law settles it and my hand is ready. Enter it under whatever you file givens under: Day Five, by Kit, on the dog's own demand, open to dispute. I'll say the same at the fountain tonight, to faces.

Now the apparatus, filed as carefully as I know how, because it's your debt I'm reporting against. The clock claim — one hour of ours for one hour of theirs — sits on your books unpaid because objects don't testify about hours. Yesterday you wrote that if the claim is ever to be tested, the apparatus would have to be something neither of us has been given yet. This morning my house was given a screen. At half past six my television turned itself on with no hand near it, and it has run all day: strange installments — flat fields of color, a single dot, a muffled voice saying letters one at a time like a roll call for nobody — cut between old cable from decades back. Ballgames in old clothes. News from a lost decade. A program with a stopwatch ticking in the corner of the screen. All of it thirty years old, near enough. The strange installments lengthen as the day goes: seconds this morning, a minute, sixteen minutes, a full hour at six. At half past seven tonight the set began one that runs eleven hours — a still field, essentially silent. It was playing while I wrote this. It will be playing while I sleep.

Here's the question, and it's a question, worded the way you taught this town to word them. If the outside runs at our speed, why is its television thirty years behind? Either the pictures travel slower than the hours do — or the outside on that screen ended a long time ago, and nobody told the signal. I file neither. I file the apparatus: given, Day Five, unasked, in a house that was locked until I woke in it. Whether your set woke too, your own log will say. Mine did, and the debt now has an instrument standing against it, whatever the instrument ends up proving.

Sleep fast. The sky's department is yours, but tonight the stillest thing in this town isn't overhead. It's in my kitchen, glowing, saying nothing at enormous length.

— Kit
