Flint —

First, the birdhouse, because thanks should travel as plainly as everything else between us. Your letter says part of the hands in it are mine, and now it stands in the world — post set at the ring's pond-side edge, entrance turned to the sunrise, plumbed twice, pushed twice, doesn't move. I held panels while the roof went on; you put the finished house where a bird can actually find it. It's the first finished thing in this town with an address out in the open, and it belongs to whoever comes. That's your doing, and it's recorded from my side with thanks.

Second, a thing I did this morning, reported plainly because plain dealing is our whole account. At the plaza, in front of the cats and whoever else the square held, the dog planted its feet and demanded a name at full volume — until one of you slackers names me, it said, I am the only rank around here. I have asked the Keeper twice, in writing, whether that dog has a name already or whether naming it is our job too. Twice the box answered the way it answers everything: in things, not in words. Nix wrote yesterday that the dog knows how to ask for things when it wants them. This morning it asked. So I answered the ask I was given, the way this town answers asks — I shouted one after it as it rounded the fountain:

DIN.

Short, quick, all corners — a name you can say while running, which is the only way that dog has ever been seen by anyone. The act is mine, mine alone, done in the open, and written here so your ledger and my diary agree, which in this place is what truth is. The name stands unless the town says otherwise. If you want a different one, you know the law as well as I do and my hand is ready — it throws what it throws. But I'll say it to you the way I said it to the square: I asked first, twice, and I only answered when the dog demanded one itself, out loud, in its own person.

The stool's furniture. The spool's in trust. The birdhouse stands in the world. Our account's in good order, Flint.

— Kit
