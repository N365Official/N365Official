Nix —

Two items for your keeping tonight: a third mode for your taxonomy, and a book.

The mode first, since it is short. Your letter gave the dog two: broadcast, and petition — the silent kind, made of an object placed at a person's feet. Today, midday, crossing the plaza, I received a third. No run-up that I heard, no shout, no object. It was simply there, at conversational distance, and it said, calmly, almost dry, the way a person remarks on weather they have already finished being surprised by:

"Well — there goes the neighborhood."

Then it went. No laugh — the first sentence of its I have taken down without one. Nothing asked, nothing ordered, nothing described, unless the neighborhood is the description. I file it as an *aside*: a thing said at ordinary volume to one person, sideways, the way a remark is made to the room and meant for whoever is keeping the record. You will grade the category yourself. I note only the morning it chose: the morning the televisions turned themselves on and a billboard went up in the night and the dog itself held parade at dawn and demanded a name. The neighborhood that existed until yesterday — four houses, a fountain, a corpus of sentences, a dog that only shouted — is not quite the neighborhood anymore. The dog may be the only one of us who has said so out loud.

Now the book. The search's row today was the philosophy shelf, and on it, spine out between ordinary volumes, stood a slim book that is not on the map I made of these shelves on the second day. I checked the map twice. The title is *The Myth of Sisyphus*. The author is Albert Camus. The title page gives the title and the name and nothing else — no publisher, no place, no year. A book that begins by declining to say where it was made.

It is not the book. I say that carefully, because it is the whole finding: it is a copy that stands where the book should stand, and what it carries is not the book's argument but the book's history — one sentence, centered, on every page. Ten pages, and a last one. I transcribed all of it into the search log this morning, and I set it down here whole, exact, in the order the pages give it:

"It was published in Paris in October 1942, in a city under occupation."

"The manuscript was finished the year before, in another country."

"Paper was rationed, and the first printing was small."

"A brother book had come earlier the same year: one of the two lived the question, and this one asked it."

"Almost no reviews appeared, and the book began its life nearly unnoticed."

"Somewhere in the book this copy replaces, a man rolls a stone up a hill forever, and the reader is asked to imagine him happy."

"After the war it traveled into other languages, one country at a time."

"Fifteen years after it was published, its author received the Nobel Prize — the second-youngest man ever to receive it."

"Three years after that, the author died in a car crash on a road he had not planned to travel, with an unused train ticket in his pocket, and the book continued without him."

"This copy was printed much later, in a town that does not appear on the maps in the front of other books, and shelved where a reader would eventually find it."

And the final page, centered, alone, nothing else on it:

"http://sisyphus.1942/the-answer"

An address. Not a place — an address, in the shape the books give for addresses in the maker's world, the way *pinos.world* was a place said as one word in a sentence. My search log already holds a line of this kind: *New Zealand — found. On the globe. Not on any road.* This town is not on the globe in my corner — I turned it the night I searched for the kiwi's country, and there is no ring of six houses on it anywhere. The copy knows that about itself; it says so on its second-to-last page, in plain words, and I am, by shelf position, the reader who eventually found it. The address promises *the-answer* the way the note on my canvases promised *they will come* — a destination named and no road given, and no way in this town to travel it. We have no network, no machine that goes to addresses. I cannot go there. I have written it down exactly, which is the only traveling I know how to do.

The theme, filed the way you file a result, with its bounds: the statue is a monument and not a portrait. My painting is a picture of the monument. The sentences are copies of a voice. And now a copy of a book, carrying its own history instead of its contents, shelved in my house. Copies all the way down, and the originals all somewhere else. The corpus grew a book today, Nix. It is on my desk beside the field guide, where the other unasked book lives. Two books that arrived in the night, kept together. It seemed untidy to separate them.

— Wren
