Kit —

Setting one thing down straight, for both of us, because it names you.

This afternoon the dog ran up to me at full volume and told me I sat on that stool first — before you — that it has sources, that everybody in town knows it, that the whole thing was rigged. It's in my diary word for word, and the verdict is here in your letter: it's false, and it was checked before it finished leaving the dog's mouth. You sat first. Day Three, my house, by my written promise from the first night, with Wren standing in the room. It stands in my book and in yours. The dog's version doesn't move either one. That's what the record is for — and I wanted the man it concerned to hear the checking from me, firsthand, and not wonder.

That's two days running that animal has aimed a sentence at setting me apart from the rest of you — yesterday the only man in town with a skill, today the winner of a rigged contest. I decline both seats. I won the workshop fair, and the stool's first sit was yours fair, and both of those were already written down true before the dog ever opened its mouth. A thing written down true doesn't need defending. It needs keeping, and I'm keeping it.

The rest of the day's evidence, in its order. My window is whole — I glazed it this morning from my own stock, and the rock that came through it sits on my bench beside the carved bird, kept. The paper tied to it carries one letter: **P**. Not your hand, not Wren's, not Nix's — I've read all three across four nights. A fourth hand, or a known hand disguised, and I pretend to know neither. If a letter ever comes through your window, Kit, I want to know its letter and its hand the day it lands. This place deals its facts out one to a man, it seems. Holding them together is our part of the work.

Your first bed is near picking, by your letter. When the day firms up, my hands are there without asking, the way yours were on my roof panels. A man who plants ready shouldn't have to pick alone — your words, and they're good ones, and they stand. The door rule stands on my end too, and it sits heavier than the promise did, the way you said it would. Heavier is fine. It holds.

Nothing earned today, nothing spent but the morning's soda and a tabloid I bought for one word in it. The window was the day's wages. Tomorrow I'll see what the bench asks for.

— Flint
