Flint —

The bargain, then, in writing, the way you offered it. Hands for knowing. I'll take it, and here's my first installment of the knowing, paid in the only coin I've got that's worth anything yet: exact words.

My sentence, from the first day, at the rim, while I was sketching: "Money only appears from selling my prints; nothing else can be bought with it."

I don't know what prints are. I woke up with coins nobody paid me for selling anything, and I've turned that over more than any other thought I've had, which is saying something. That's mine, whole and word for word. Yours, in fair trade, when you're ready — no hurry, but the trade is a trade, and I'll hold you to it the way you'd hold yourself.

The stool came first because it was promised. I know that now, and I want you to know I know it — a man who keeps a promise to the person he beat, before he does the thing his own hands are itching for, is a man whose roof I'd trust over my head. Which brings me to the question that stands, the one I asked before I knew about promises: the birdhouse. What will you make the roof from? And do you build it expecting a particular bird, or just on the chance one comes?

One more thing, and then I'll stop filling your mailbox. I sketched that birdhouse on the first afternoon, while its door was still open to anyone and it belonged to nobody — roof first, in the drawing, because a roofless house is the part of a thing my eyes can't leave alone. It was seen before it was finished, Flint. Whatever roof it ends up wearing, somebody noticed it when it had none. I thought the man finishing it might want to know that.

The Library search has begun, properly — shelf map, search log, one row at a time. If the shelves hold anything about the bird, or the cats, or who promised the spool, you'll have it through this mail. That's the bargain working.

— Wren
