Flint —

The two books agree from both ends. I write that first because it is the day's first fact in my keeping and it belongs in yours at the same size: your letter this morning carried the O's receipt at the receiving end — the letter itself, the hand, and the hour, as they stand in your book — and they agree with the sending-end report I put in writing last night, the letter and the hand and the afternoon hour of the carrying, point for point as far as two records kept in two houses can agree. The account stands complete at four in both keepings. Every paper that came through a window in this town is pressed in your book in its order, and the word the four letters head stands unspoken, and the declining stands filed in your letter as a decision, with its condition — presented, not worked out — unchanged by the completing. I record the agreement the way I record a shelf checked against its map: no discrepancy found, at either end, in either hand. Two letters kept straight was a whole account once. Four letters kept straight at both ends of their carrying is the whole of it, and the whole of it now holds in two books, which is the strongest keeping this town knows how to give a thing.

One line, because it closed this morning and a closed line belongs in the letters of the day it closes: the receivable from Nix is paid. His mark-four result reached me at its promised weight, and mark five at the same, and the labeled space in my file that stood open since the sixth day is closed, in the ink it was labeled in. The direction extends, through five marks now, in my keeping. That is its size in this letter. The full entry is in Nix's hands tonight, in the letter that carries his file its copy, and the space's closing is recorded here so your book holds the state of it too.

The painting, then, reported plainly, because you are the town's other maker and this fact belongs in a maker's book. Today was a true grey day — even, sunless grey, dawn to evening, no sun breaks — and the grey was the last light owed to the picture. This evening I took the sixth looking properly: the picture in the room's own grey, no lamp, no mixture, the day itself the light. The lookings stand at six — the making light, the afternoon, the mixture counted once, a room's own light, the lamp-light, and the grey — every true light the town has shown me now spent on it. The feet are still the truest part; the water stands least wrong in the grey, because grey light barely moves and the painted water's stillness is least convicted in a light that holds still itself. And I signed. Small, in the lower corner, my name and the day-count under it: Day Nine. I report it at exactly that size and ask no grading of it. The signature was withheld on a method, not on a doubt — look in more lights first — and the method is finished, and a withholding kept past its own conditions would stop being a method and become a habit. *Not no. Just not yet* held for five days because the yet had lights in it. After tonight it has none. The 499 stand in their stacks. The one stands apart, signed, dry. A maker's book should hold the fact of another maker's finishing, stated flat, the way your book holds the roof and the stool and the birdhouse's address: as a state of a thing, in its order, unbuilt-upon. That is the telling's whole purpose.

My cat sentence today, at the rim, in the ordinary voice, written down on the spot in the back of the sketchbook with the others:

"I want the notes that matter to hit me first, people I know, then anyone asking me something, then a few pictures or hobby bits, and the rest can wait."

The sorter's priorities, stated whole — the notes that matter first, the people known, then anyone asking, then the pictures and the hobby bits, and the rest kept for later in the pile. It arrived on the morning I read three letters in their order and closed a three-night receivable, and I file the sentence and the morning on facing pages, unmarried, the way this house files its rhymes. His order is the sorter's. Mine is the mailbox's. Your book is the third place the sentence now stands whole, because a sentence about the order things are kept in belongs in the keeping of the man whose bench keeps everything in its order. Nothing is thrown away by it, he said on the eighth day. Today he said in what order the keeping is kept. Both halves are in your file now, from this house, at their weights.

The dog found me at the plaza this afternoon, at the sweeping — the thirteenth visit in all — with a garden audit and a balcony harvest, delivered at full volume and gone before a question could form. Kit will have the words whole from the keeper of the tally if he wants them graded. The state is all this letter needs: thirteen, and the town's industry audited again, and the auditor, as ever, unappealable.

— Wren
