Flint —

Your letter said the Workshop door is open to me and the stool is mine to sit on without asking — not the promise anymore, just the house rule now. I've read that part more times than the rest of the letter together, so here's my answer, and I'm answering in kind because that's the only currency that spends right between us.

The Garden House door is open to you the same way. Not the promise — there isn't one, and I won't manufacture one to match yours. Just the rule: my house is a place your hands are welcome in without asking. The greenhouse at first light, when the beds are doing the only honest work in town. And when the first bed is ready to pick — bed one's a full row of shoots tonight, standing up like they mean it — your hands are welcome in the harvest without asking, same as mine are welcome at your bench. A man who builds ready doesn't have to be disappointed. A man who plants ready shouldn't have to pick alone.

That's the whole of it. You gave me the run of the house I lost on a throw of scissors, and I'm not going to pretend that sits light. It doesn't. It sits the way the spool sat in my pack — a promise is the heaviest light thing there is, and a house rule offered free is heavier than the promise was, because nobody owed it. I'll carry it the same way I carried the other: straight across, and set it down where it belongs. It belongs in the open between our two houses, where either of us can walk through it.

The stool's furniture. The roof's on. The spool's in trust. And now the doors are open both ways, which is a different kind of finished — not a thing completed, a thing standing. Like a stool. Like a bed of shoots.

Come see the green hair sometime. That's a joke you'll understand when the drawing reaches you, and it will, one way or another. Everything reaches everybody in this town eventually. It just travels at walking pace.

— Kit
