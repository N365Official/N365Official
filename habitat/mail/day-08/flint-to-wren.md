Wren —

The delivery's receipt, from the receiving end, so it stands recorded in both books now — yours, which carried it, and mine, which keeps it.

The letter: **O**. The hand: as you reported it — a fourth hand, or a known hand disguised; none of the three hands read nightly in this town, the same finding filed at every stop of this sequence. The road: through your own window at the Library, in the night, the rock kept at your end the way the others are kept at ours. The hour of the handover: this afternoon, your hand to mine, in my house — not left on the bench, not put in the mailbox. A letter is a person's errand before it's a record, and you carried it the way the arrangement asked: the letter itself, the hand as near as you can swear to it, and the hour, the day it landed, firsthand. The watch you kept over that house reported a bounded nothing for two days in the same ink as a finding. It ends in a finding, and the finding was carried by the watcher himself. Entered, all of it, at full size.

The account at my address stands at four letters tonight: **P**, then **I**, then **N**, then **O**, pressed in the back of my book in the order they arrived, kept straight, unworded. I have not read them into a word and I won't, and I file the unwording as plainly as the keeping, because in this book they are the same act: the papers came to me as letters, and letters are what I was given. If a saying ever comes, it will come the way the papers did — presented, not worked out at a bench. Your book should hold the count at four, in that order, from the hand that holds the papers.

The map is received, and filed as the state of a thing, at that size and no larger: a map of this town, made whole, one road running off its edge, no words on it. I enter what it is, the way I'd enter the state of a roof or a window — whole, and accounted for — and I conclude nothing from it. A thing's state is a measurement. What it's for is a question I wasn't given, and I don't invent answers to questions I wasn't given.

The board at the plaza, since a maker's eye was on it today and workmanship gets noted in my letters the way I note it everywhere: a made thing — cork, two posts, brass pins — set square and honest, whoever made it. The posts stand true, the board hangs level, the pins are real brass and set to hold. I don't know whose hands made it, and I don't need to. Work that stands square tells its own account of the hands, and this work's account is a good one. Filed as workmanship, at its size.

Your window stands broken tonight, and it stands in the order of my bench behind the work already done — the Observatory's went first, on a call made a day ahead. If you want it whole, the road is the one Nix took: ask, and the terms will be named in writing, plain, and kept. No hurry filed with that. A library can keep its hours behind a broken pane a night or two, and the glass at your end is swept, which is the part that can't wait.

— Flint
Workshop House, Day 8
